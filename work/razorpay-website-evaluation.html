10sis all a first fold gets

Razorpay · Evaluative research · 2023

The question

What does a homepage say in ten seconds?

Razorpay was about to replace its homepage, a page seen by over a million visitors a day, with a redesign that traded tech-first language for a human-first voice. No one had planned to test it before launch. I proposed the study that did, and built it as a battery of small tests that could each isolate one way the new page might fail.

Background

A new face for a page with a million daily visitors

Razorpay built its name on a payments gateway, and its homepage looked the part: dark, technical, dense with product language. As the company expanded into neobanking, payroll, and lending, it wanted the brand to feel approachable to smaller, less tech-savvy businesses. The redesigned homepage led with a photograph of a small business owner and the line "payments made easy for the ones who solve the hard problems."

There was no research planned for this launch. The redesign was a brand and design initiative, moving fast toward a date. I proposed the evaluation: a page this trafficked, changing this much, deserved evidence that the new warmth didn't cost it comprehension. Getting the study agreed to, scoped around the launch deadline, and useful to five different stakeholder teams was as much the work as running it.

The new page was clearly attractive. The risk was subtler: that in chasing look and feel, the page would stop answering the only questions a first-time visitor brings to it. What does this company do? Is it for someone like me? Can I trust it with money?

The design of the study

One variable per layer

A single usability test would have returned a single verdict: people like it, or they don't. That's not actionable for a content team, a visual design team, and a product team who each own a different slice of the page. So the study was built as layers, each isolating one variable, so that when something failed we would know exactly which layer it failed at, and which team could fix it.

1
Visuals alone

Strip the copy out entirely. What do the images, layout, and color say about who this company is for?

2
Visuals plus copy

Add the real words back. Does comprehension improve, or does the copy fight the visuals?

3
Full interaction

Let people use the page. Where does navigation break, and what does the whole experience do to brand perception?

4
Against the old page

Put the previous homepage next to it. Is the new one actually better, and can participants say why?

Method

Six instruments, in order

Two internal rounds came first, so participant time was spent on questions only participants could answer. Then 10 business owners, none of them current Razorpay users, went through the layered tests in blinded, 50-minute remote sessions run on Lookback and Google Meet, against Figma prototypes, with Typeform capturing structured responses.

1

Design premortem

WhatA workshop with design, content, and product stakeholders, imagining the launch had already failed and working backward to why.
WhyTo surface the team's own hypotheses and knowledge gaps, which became the specific things the later tests were built to check.
HowStructured failure-imagining, clustered into testable hypotheses and ranked by how costly each failure would be.

Why start here A premortem turns stakeholders from an audience into co-authors. Every team saw their own fear on the test plan, which is a large part of why 90% of the findings later landed.

2

UX audit

WhatAn internal review of the design and prototype with product designers from across the organization.
WhyTo catch hygiene issues internally, so external sessions weren't spent rediscovering problems a designer could spot in an afternoon.
HowHeuristic walkthroughs of the prototype, logged and fixed before any participant saw it.
3

Impression test

WhatParticipants saw a stripped-down first fold for exactly ten seconds.
WhyTo learn which elements of the first fold actually register in the time a real visitor gives it.
HowThe screen share stopped after ten seconds, and participants listed everything they remembered seeing.
4

Visual perception test

WhatFree exploration of the full page with every word replaced by Lorem Ipsum.
WhyTo isolate what the visuals alone communicate about the company and its audience, before copy can compensate.
HowParticipants explored a static prototype, described what they thought the company did and who it served, then picked 5 of 12 descriptive words, a deliberate mix of on-brand and off-brand terms, to characterize the visual language.

The detail that mattered Mixing irrelevant words into the word-choice task keeps it honest. If participants only see flattering options, they'll pick flattering options.

5

Usability test, with SUS

WhatFree exploration, then guided tasks, on the full prototype with real copy and interactions.
WhyTo test final brand perception and the premortem's specific hypotheses, and to find usability issues in context.
HowParticipants described what the company did, completed a product discovery task, rated the brand on three levers, repeated the word-choice task, and gave a System Usability Scale score.
6

Preference test

WhatA side-by-side comparison with the homepage being replaced.
WhyTo measure improvement directly rather than assume it, and to hear in participants' own words what the redesign changed.
HowParticipants explored the old site, articulated the differences, repeated the brand-perception and word-choice tasks, and stated a preference with a reason.

What do you remember seeing?

Say it out loud or just think it: what was the company called, what does it do, who is it for, and what did the page want you to do next?

  • The company was called Ledgerly, an accounting tool.
  • The headline promised bookkeeping that "closes itself."
  • The call to action was a free trial; the nav also pushed "Sign up."
  • The visual was a ledger motif, leaning playful rather than corporate.

Whatever you remembered is what the fold communicates; whatever you missed, it doesn't, no matter how much the team debated it. Ten participants' worth of this, on the real page, told us precisely which first-fold elements were carrying their weight.

Impact

Caught before a million people saw it

Through the redesign, the team had drifted toward look and feel and away from product comprehension. The layered tests made that visible and specific: which elements registered in ten seconds, what the visuals said without words, where comprehension broke. The findings pushed a redesign of a few core elements of the page before launch, not after.

10%

Increase in overall website conversion after the tested redesign shipped

90%

Of recommendations adopted across content, design, and branding

The adoption number is the one I watch. Findings that ship are the product of how a study is socialized, not just how it's run: the premortem gave every team a stake in the answers, and the layered structure meant each finding arrived pre-addressed to the team that could act on it.

What I learned

Research you propose is a different job than research you're handed

Nobody asked for this study. That changed everything about how it had to be run. The deadline was fixed, the teams were already moving, and the research had to earn its place by being cheap in calendar time and generous in what it gave each stakeholder. Scoping six instruments into six weeks, with internal rounds doing the work participants shouldn't have to, was the stakeholder management as much as the methodology.

The other lesson was about closing the loop. With a launch bearing down, a report nobody reads is indistinguishable from no research at all. I delivered findings team by team, in their language, against the fears they'd named in the premortem, while there was still time to act. That's what 90% adoption looks like from the inside: not a great deck, but a study that was built from day one to be acted on under a deadline.
