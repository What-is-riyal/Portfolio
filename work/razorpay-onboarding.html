Razorpay · Design + research · 2021–22

The question

Why did merchants give up before their first payment?

Razorpay's onboarding form was where small merchants went to start accepting payments, and where a painful share of them stopped. For shop owners with low tech literacy, a 14-minute compliance form full of words like "proprietorship" was a wall. I redesigned it around a different mental model: not a form, but a conversation with a good bank clerk.

Background

A compliance form standing between a shop and its money

To accept payments through Razorpay, a merchant first had to complete onboarding: business details, regulatory KYC, bank information, spread across a tabbed, dense form that took around 14 minutes when everything went well. For the small and medium merchants signing up, a kirana store owner, a home baker, a small exporter, things often didn't go well. The form spoke in the language of compliance, and they ran businesses, not paperwork.

The cost was visible in two places: merchants who abandoned onboarding and never took their first payment, and a steady stream of support tickets from those who pushed through and got stuck.

The evidence

Listening to the form fail

There was no research team attached to this project. So before redesigning anything, I built the evidence base myself, from sources that were already there for the reading.

1

150+ support tickets, coded

I read and categorized over 150 onboarding support tickets. They mapped exactly where the form broke people: business-type jargon that didn't match how merchants describe themselves, buttons that stayed disabled with no explanation, and silence after submission.

2

50+ session recordings

Hotjar recordings of real merchants moving through the form showed what tickets couldn't: the long pauses on the business-type question, the back-and-forth between tabs looking for something familiar, the rage clicks on a disabled button, the exits.

Why both sources Tickets are written by people who didn't give up. The recordings showed the ones who did, and where.

3

A teardown workshop

I ran an empathy workshop with designers and PMs, walking the current flow screen by screen against the ticket and recording evidence. The team annotated every screen with what a low-tech-literacy merchant would be thinking at that moment, which turned an abstract drop-off number into specific, fixable moments.

4

Competitive analysis, 8 players

I compared onboarding across Razorpay's competitors, from global players to Indian ones, on what they asked, when they asked it, and in what tone. The pattern: the friendliest flows asked for less up front, asked conversationally, and explained why each piece of information was needed.

The turn

How would a bank clerk do this?

The reframe came from an exercise, not a screen. These merchants had all opened bank accounts before. That process also involves KYC, PAN numbers, and proof documents, and yet they completed it, because a person walked them through it. So we role-played it: one person as the bank clerk, one as a merchant, acting out how an account gets opened across the counter.

The clerk never hands you a wall of fields. They ask one thing at a time, in plain words, tell you why they need each document, and react to your answers. The merchants' mental model for "giving my details to a financial institution" already existed. The form just didn't match it. The redesign's job was to behave like the clerk.

Design principles

Three rules from the counter

The evidence and the role play condensed into three principles that governed every screen of the new flow.

1

One question per screen

Each screen asks exactly one thing, the way a clerk would. Progress is always visible, so the merchant knows how much counter is left.

2

Say why you're asking

Every sensitive request carries a plain-language reason. "We require this to verify your identity. This information will not be stored by us." Trust is built line by line, not in a privacy policy.

3

Speak merchant, not compliance

Questions use the words merchants use for their own businesses. The regulatory mapping happens behind the screen, where it belongs.

Validation and impact

Tested with eleven merchants, then shipped

Before launch, I ran a usability test of the new flow with 11 merchants against the old one. 73% preferred the redesigned flow, and the sessions caught wording that still read as jargon, which we fixed before shipping. The flow went through multiple iterations against this feedback rather than launching the first version that tested well.

An honest note on the role

This was my first role at Razorpay, as a product designer. The research here, tickets, recordings, workshops, the role play, the usability test, was research I did because the design needed it, not because anyone had scoped a study. It's also the project that convinced me to move into research full time: the redesign worked exactly to the degree that the evidence under it was right.

What I learned

Users already have a mental model. Find it before you invent one

The breakthrough in this project came from noticing that merchants had already succeeded at this exact task, at a bank counter, and asking what that experience had that our form lacked. Since then, whenever a flow is failing, my first research question is where users have done this successfully before, and what carried them through it there.

The other lesson was about evidence you don't have to commission. Support tickets and session recordings were sitting there, unread, describing the problem in users' own words. A lot of research debt is really just reading debt.
