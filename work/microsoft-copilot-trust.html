Microsoft · 2024–25

A point of view for Copilot in Outlook

Microsoft was integrating Copilot into Outlook one capability at a time, and each capability had its own research behind it. What the organization lacked was a single position on what all that evidence meant. I synthesized a year of studies into a white paper on what users need before they trust an AI assistant inside their inbox, and what that implies about what to build first.

Role
UX Researcher, contract via Kadence
Timeline
Sept 2024 – Feb 2025
Working with
Khyati Seth, lead researcher, Outlook UXR
Inputs
A year of internal studies, plus public social listening
Deliverable
A point-of-view white paper with design guidelines and a feature checklist

This work is under NDA. I describe my method, the structure of the argument, and the reasoning behind it. Internal findings, feature names, and roadmap details are not shown, and this page has no internal artifacts on it for that reason.

Context

Copilot's capabilities in Outlook, drafting, summarizing, scheduling, prioritizing, were each being developed by a different feature team, and each team had commissioned its own research: deep dives, focus groups, concept tests, accumulating for over a year. Every study was competent on its own terms. But each one was scoped to the feature that paid for it, so the body of work as a whole disagreed with itself in places, repeated itself in others, and never answered the question underneath all of them: taken together, what were users saying about living with an AI assistant?

The organization asked for a point-of-view paper: one document that turned the accumulated evidence into a position the whole team could build against, or argue with. I authored that paper, working closely with the lead researcher. The work was a mix of research and editorial judgment: deciding which findings were load-bearing, which were artifacts of how one study happened to be framed, and what the evidence supported when read as a whole.

How I approached the synthesis

Synthesis across studies has failure modes of its own, and the method was designed around them. The biggest risk in combining a year of reports is laundering: a weak finding from one small study gets averaged into the pile and comes out the other side looking like consensus. So I treated the source reports the way I would treat participants, each one read closely, questioned, and coded before it was allowed to influence the argument. The working board ended up holding roughly 85 coded notes across the source studies.

Concretely, the process ran in five steps:

  1. Extract, study by study. I pulled every finding, open question, and recurring complaint out of each report into its own cluster, keeping the source attached to every note. I deliberately did no theme-building at this stage, because mixing sources too early is exactly how weak evidence acquires false weight.
  2. Interrogate provenance. A finding only moved forward once I could answer where it came from, what the sample was, and what was actually measured. My notes from this stage are mostly questions rather than claims, and answering them did more for the paper's credibility than any framework did.
  3. Look for repetition across unrelated studies. The signal that justifies a white paper is the pattern no single study could see. The same anxieties about accuracy, the same demand for control, and the same frustration with unexplained decisions kept surfacing in studies that had nothing else in common, different methods, different features, different participants. Those repetitions became the paper's spine.
  4. Pressure-test the themes externally. Internal studies share internal blind spots, so I checked the emerging themes against public social listening: what people were saying about AI assistants on forums and in the press. Where the outside conversation echoed the internal data, I trusted the theme more; where it didn't, I went back to the sources. Social listening is noisy and self-selected, so I used it only to test themes, never to generate them.
  5. Re-sort the evidence under the argument. I drafted the paper's outline, then re-binned every note under the section it supported. Evidence that fit nowhere either earned a new section or was cut. The final structure matched that re-sorted outline almost exactly, which gave me some confidence the argument was carrying the evidence rather than decorating it.

What the paper argued

The paper's central claim is that assistance deepens in stages, and that trust is built or destroyed depending on whether the product respects the order of those stages.

  1. On-demand. The user asks; the assistant answers; the user reviews the output before using it. Summarize this thread, draft a reply, find that email. Across the studies, this is where most people's comfort actually sat, regardless of where the product vision hoped they were.
  2. Proactive. The assistant surfaces summaries, suggestions, and priorities without being asked, but every action still belongs to the user. This step is larger than it looks: the assistant now has to judge what is worth interrupting someone for, and to explain its reasoning, or it reads as noise and gets switched off.
  3. Automatic. The assistant acts on its own, sending, archiving, rescheduling, within boundaries the user has set. The paper argued that a task should only reach this stage after the assistant has proven itself on the two below, for that task specifically. Skipping stages is where trust collapses.

Underneath the staged model sit three prerequisites, drawn from where trust consistently broke down across the studies. Wherever a feature was failing users, at least one of these was missing:

  • Transparency. People need to see what the assistant considered and why it acted. Inside a black box, even small mistakes generalize into a refusal to trust the assistant with anything that matters. With an explanation attached, the same mistake becomes correctable.
  • Control. Correction and boundary-setting have to live in the moment of use. Control that exists only in a settings page functionally does not exist, because nobody visits it at the moment the assistant gets something wrong.
  • Learnability. Nobody in the studies expected the assistant to be perfect. They expected it to improve, toward their tone, their priorities, their habits. An assistant that repeats a corrected mistake teaches people to stop correcting it, and the relationship never deepens enough to support autonomy.

One recurring test case made the framework concrete for stakeholders: when every app runs its own assistant with its own memory, the user ends up re-introducing themselves, their role, their projects, their preferences, in each app separately. The framework describes that as a transparency failure and a learnability failure at the same time, and it is a problem no single feature team can fix alone. Giving every team the same vocabulary for cross-cutting problems like this one was a large part of the reason to write one paper instead of six readouts.

The deliverable

The paper was structured so teams could act on it, or push back on it, without me in the room:

  • A stated position on which user needs are load-bearing, which stage of assistance each opportunity area belongs to, and what each feature must demonstrate before moving to a deeper stage.
  • Design guidelines that translate transparency, control, and learnability from principles into implementation guidance a feature team could apply to its own surface.
  • A pre-ship checklist covering transparency, accuracy, learning behavior, user control, discoverability, and risk, written so any team could run it without research support.
  • Named gaps. Where the evidence was thin, the paper says so and recommends the studies to run next. A synthesis that hides its own limits invites teams to overtrust it, which would eventually be discovered, and the paper's authority would go with it.

The paper shipped in the final weeks of my contract, so I did not get to watch its recommendations move through roadmaps, and the NDA covers what happened afterward. I would rather state that plainly than imply an outcome I can't substantiate. What I can stand behind is the part this page documents: how the synthesis was built, and whether the reasoning holds.

Reflection

This was the first project where my primary contribution was synthesis rather than fieldwork, and it changed how I rate that skill. The hardest moments were recognizing when several studies were independently describing the same underlying problem from different angles, and then naming that problem precisely enough that the name could travel between teams without losing its meaning.

It also gave me the frame I now carry into AI governance work: trust in automated systems builds in stages, with transparency and control as the floor, and autonomy as something granted task by task once the earlier stages hold. That connection between everyday product research and questions of AI oversight is the territory I want to keep working in.
