Microsoft · Mixed-methods retention study · 2024

The question

Why were people leaving Outlook on the mobile browser?

Usage of Outlook's lightweight mobile web experience was declining faster than expected among consumer users, and everyone on the team had a different explanation. Thirty-six interviews and a validation survey later, the answer lived somewhere nobody had been looking: in the whole system of email accounts each person quietly runs, and the specific job this one endpoint does inside it.

The setup

Everyone had a theory. Nobody had the same one.

Leadership, PMs, and engineers each carried their own hypothesis about the decline. Before fielding anything, the team pooled what was already known, secondary research across data science reports, UX audits, marketing reports, and earlier studies, and ran a hypothesis workshop to separate what we believed from what we knew.

What was left, the gaps no existing data could answer, became the research plan. That workshop also produced the six user segments, drawn from usage behavior across Outlook's endpoints, that structured everything downstream.

The approach

Qual to find the story, quant to check it

01

Hypothesis workshop

Secondary research synthesized and presented to the whole team, with data scientists, PMs, and designers in the room. Knowledge gaps, not opinions, set the research objectives.

02

36 moderated interviews

One-on-one sessions, 4 to 6 participants from each of the six segments, recruited against real usage patterns. Leaders and PMs were invited to listen in live, so findings landed before the report did.

03

Validation survey, 300+ responses

A survey, designed from a survey map of the interview findings, checked the qualitative story against 300+ respondents in each cohort before the team treated it as direction.

The turn · accounts have life cycles

Stage 1 of 4

Primary

The main account. Checked daily, guarded carefully, attached to everything that matters. This is the account product teams imagine when they say "the user."

Stage 2 of 4

Secondary

The alternate. Signups, backups, the overflow. Checked on purpose, but less often, and usually from whichever endpoint is closest at hand rather than a favorite app.

Stage 3 of 4

Tertiary

The junk drawer. Newsletters, promotions, spam, the occasional one-time code. Often a former primary that was shared too widely for too many years. Nobody redesigns their way out of this stage.

Stage 4 of 4

Abandoned

Dormant, but never deleted. Opened once a year for a password reset, if the password still works. Where an account sits in this cycle predicts how, and how often, any endpoint will be used to reach it.

The finding that reframed the product

People route each account to the endpoint that fits its job

Loyalty to a favorite email client barely showed up in the data. What showed up instead was routing: people match the endpoint to the account's purpose, a dedicated app for the account they live in, a quick browser tab for the junk drawer they only visit for a one-time code.

That gave the lightweight web client a role nobody had been designing for: the buffer. It catches people when their main app is slow, unsynced, or down. It serves people on storage-constrained phones who can't afford a heavy app. Its retention is partly tied to the reliability of products it doesn't control, which is exactly why "why are people leaving?" couldn't be answered by looking at this product alone.

What it changed

Findings that arrived before the report did

  • aLeaders heard users firsthand. The whole team, leadership included, was invited to listen in on live interviews, so the evidence didn't wait for a readout to start working.
  • bDeep-dive readouts with design, PM, and engineering walked each function through the sections that affected their decisions.
  • cTop findings traveled up and out: presented to leadership in India and to the global Outlook teams at headquarters.
  • dA tracker, not just a deck. Insights were handed to PMs as a working sheet for semester planning, and most of them influenced the next semester's plan for the mobile web experience.
Whose work this was

This was a team study led by Khyati Seth (lead researcher), with researcher and PM partners. I joined as a contract researcher: I contributed to the discussion guide, ran interview sessions alongside Khyati, led the qualitative analysis under her guidance, and wrote the first drafts of the report that she refined. The account life cycle lens on this page comes from that analysis. The segmentation and study design credit belongs to the whole team.

What I learned

Some retention questions belong to the user's whole system

It's tempting to treat retention as something to fix with better onboarding or a redesigned inbox. For a product that sits inside someone's decades-old personal email ecosystem, the honest unit of analysis is that ecosystem: where this one piece fits, and what would have to be true for it to matter more. Choosing that unit of analysis early shaped the discussion guide, the segments, and ultimately what the team did with the findings.

The output I keep thinking about is the shared vocabulary. The account life cycle and the segments let a cross-functional team talk about a person who checks a near-abandoned account once a year and a person who lives in the client daily, without flattening them into one persona. That vocabulary kept the findings alive long after the readouts ended.
