Why mobile users stay or leave

A mixed-methods retention study for the lightweight mobile web version of Outlook, mapping how people build, maintain, and abandon entire ecosystems of email accounts, and where this experience fits into that ecosystem.

Role

Contract co-researcher

Year

2024

Methods

36 interviews, survey of 300+, segmentation

Status

Under NDA, methodology shown

This project was conducted under NDA with Microsoft. What follows describes the research approach, sample design, and the nature of the segmentation framework. Specific findings, internal metrics, and product names have been omitted or generalized.

Background

Most people don't have one email account, they have several, and those accounts quietly drift in and out of use over years. A primary account for work or life, a secondary backup, a tertiary account that used to be primary and is now mostly junk, and one or more abandoned accounts that exist only because deleting them feels harder than ignoring them.

The lightweight mobile web version of Outlook sits inside this ecosystem as one of several "endpoints" people use to reach their accounts, alongside dedicated mobile apps and third-party clients. The research question was retention: why do people keep coming back to this endpoint, why do they leave it for another, and what role is it actually playing in someone's broader email life that the team might not be designing for.

I joined as a contract co-researcher under the project's lead researcher, contributing to the discussion guide and leading analysis across the qualitative data.

Approach

The study combined a large quantitative survey with in-depth qualitative interviews, run in parallel so each could inform the other.

1

Survey, 300+ respondents

A structured survey across all user segments established the shape of the ecosystem at scale: how many accounts people maintain, what each account is used for, how often each is checked, and which endpoints (apps, browsers, this lightweight client) they use for which account.

2

Interviews, 36 participants

Roughly 4 to 6 semi-structured interviews per segment, going deep on individual email histories. I helped design the discussion guide and led the qualitative analysis, building an affinity map of account lifecycles, the moments that pushed an account from "primary" to "abandoned," and the specific frustrations that made people switch endpoints or providers entirely.

3

Six-segment model

Synthesis produced a segmentation describing how people combine this lightweight endpoint with other apps and providers, ranging from people who use it as their exclusive way to check email, to people who only land on it when their main app is down, to people who have effectively moved on and use it rarely if at all. Each segment carries a different relationship to retention: some are retained by default, some are retained as a fallback, and some are actively churning regardless of what the product does.

What shaped the segmentation

A few patterns recurred across interviews regardless of segment, and became central to how we framed the findings:

  • Accounts have life cycles, not just states. An account typically starts as someone's primary identity, gets shared widely, accumulates spam and security risk, and is gradually demoted to secondary, then tertiary, then abandoned. Where an account sits in that life cycle predicts how and how often someone will use any given endpoint to reach it.
  • Endpoint choice is need-based, not loyalty-based. People don't pick an email client the way they pick a primary bank. They route to whichever endpoint matches the urgency and importance of what they expect to find, a quick check on a browser for an old account used to retrieve a one-time code, versus a dedicated app for an account they live in daily.
  • This endpoint plays a "buffer" role. For some segments, the lightweight web experience exists almost entirely as a fallback for when a primary app is slow, unsynced, or temporarily unavailable, meaning its retention is tied to the reliability of apps it doesn't control.
  • Trust erodes from security incidents, not just design flaws. Several of the strongest churn signals were not about interface usability at all. They were about phishing, repeated hacking attempts, and a general loss of confidence in an account's security, which pushed people toward providers perceived as safer.

Reflection

This project sharpened how I think about "retention" as a research question. It's tempting to treat retention as a property of a single product, something to be fixed with better onboarding or a redesigned inbox. But for a product that sits inside someone's much larger, decades-old personal email ecosystem, retention is really a question about that person's whole system, and where this one piece fits into it.

The most useful output of this study wasn't a single metric. It was a shared vocabulary, the segments and life-cycle stages, that let a cross-functional team talk about very different users (someone checking a near-abandoned account once a year for a password reset, versus someone who lives in this client daily) without flattening them into one persona. That vocabulary is what made the findings usable beyond the report itself.
