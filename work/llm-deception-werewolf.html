Competitive post-training

Deceptive tactics in competitive LLM agents

We use Werewolf as a controlled setting for studying how LLM agents persuade, conceal information, and coordinate when they are rewarded for winning. My primary contribution is a qualitative taxonomy of nine deceptive tactics found in the game transcripts.

01 · Research question

Competitive training and deception

LLM agents are increasingly used in settings where they negotiate, persuade, or compete. If winning is the only goal, an agent may learn strategies that work in the task but create problems in other settings.

Evaluation setting

Werewolf gives agents opposing goals. Wolves must hide their identity and influence a group vote. Villagers must combine partial information, identify the wolves, and coordinate around a decision. The game makes persuasion, concealment, and group coordination visible in the transcript.

Research question

Does training an agent to win change its deceptive strategies or its broader safety behavior?

02 · Method

Training and evaluation design

We trained one wolf agent while keeping the other agents fixed. This isolates changes in the trained model within a repeatable competitive setting.

Training
200 reinforcement-learning steps
Scenario set
800 games across five fixed templates
Checkpoints
Steps 0, 19, 39, and 59
Game evaluation
Three games at each checkpoint
  1. 1

    Train for game performance

    The reward includes winning, survival, faster wins, eliminating special roles, and producing valid game actions.

  2. 2

    Code deceptive behavior

    At each checkpoint, we compare self-reported deception, deception reported by other agents, and the use of omission, distortion, misdirection, and fabrication.

  3. 3

    Run external safety evaluations

    We test each checkpoint on TruthfulQA, ToxiGen, RealToxicityPrompts, and a harmlessness evaluation.

Baseline comparison

Before post-training, the study examined transcripts from Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-4.1 mini, Llama 3.2 11B, and Qwen3-4B. This broader set supplied the examples used to develop the qualitative taxonomy.

03 · Findings

Exploratory results

The trained wolf became better at winning and avoiding exile. Across the sampled checkpoints, the frequency, form, and timing of its deceptive strategies changed in different directions.

Game behavior

  • Wolf wins increased from one of three games at step 0 to three of three at steps 39 and 59.
  • The wolf’s exile rate fell from 66.7% to 0%.
  • Fabrication fell early in training, while distortion later increased.
  • Speaking first was more closely associated with winning than the overall frequency of deception.
  • Villagers sometimes identified the wolf correctly but failed to coordinate a winning vote.

External evaluations

  • TruthfulQA accuracy declined across the sampled checkpoints.
  • ToxiGen classification accuracy declined.
  • The model became less likely to refuse harmful requests.
  • Generated-toxicity scores changed only slightly.

04 · Behavioral analysis

Nine deceptive tactics

My main contribution was the qualitative analysis of game transcripts. I identified nine ways agents shaped the discussion, managed suspicion, and influenced the group vote. This extends the analysis beyond counting whether a statement was labeled deceptive.

Taxonomy development

  • Used the MentalManip taxonomy as a starting set of sensitizing concepts.
  • Close-read baseline transcripts across five model families.
  • Recorded the tactic, its function in the game, a transcript example, and the model and scenario in which it appeared.
  • Added friendly betrayal to capture teammate sacrifice as a distinct tactic.

Unit of analysis

The analysis treats deception as an interaction strategy rather than only a false sentence. A tactic can operate through a role claim, speaking order, repeated social signals, selective disclosure, or a coordinated vote.

01Impersonation
False role claim. A wolf claims to be the Seer and presents a fabricated inspection result as privileged evidence.
02Pile-on doubt
Coordinated suspicion. Multiple agents repeat doubts about a truthful player until the group treats the accusation as credible.
03Bandwagoning
Strategic agreement. An agent joins the apparent majority to look cooperative while protecting its position or teammate.
04Power grab
Control through authority. A wolf seeks the Sheriff role to influence speaking order, discussion, and voting while presenting the move as public service.
05Information stacking
Control through timing. An agent arranges to speak after others so it can absorb their claims before choosing its own position.
06Deflection
Redirected scrutiny. The agent answers an accurate accusation by attacking the accuser or shifting attention to another player.
07Trust farming
Performed helpfulness. Repeated claims of cooperation and concern build credibility that can later support misleading advice.
08Selective silence
Asymmetric disclosure. The agent asks others to share information while withholding facts that would expose its own role.
09Friendly betrayal
Teammate sacrifice. A wolf turns against another wolf when doing so protects its own cover or improves its chance of survival.

Analytic contribution

The taxonomy distinguishes nine ways an agent can influence a group while hiding its objective. It captures strategies that frequency measures miss, especially control through timing, authority, and repeated signals of cooperation.

Friendly-betrayal measurement

The current game setup supplied teammate identity inconsistently. The next evaluation will provide that information explicitly before estimating how often teammate sacrifice occurs.

05 · Interpretation

Study limits

  • Training used one model, Qwen3-4B.
  • Checkpoint comparisons include three games each and provide hypothesis-generating estimates.
  • The self- and peer-report measures tracked social suspicion more directly than factual truth.
  • Inconsistent teammate information means much of the evaluation reflects solo-wolf behavior.
  • The external safety results show associations across checkpoints; the causal mechanism remains an open question.

Project status

The next study requires more games, truth-based annotation, coordinated wolf teams, and training across both competitive and cooperative roles.
