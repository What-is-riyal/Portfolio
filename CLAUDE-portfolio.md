# Portfolio — Project Constitution

You are redesigning the portfolio of Priyal, a researcher positioned at the intersection of UX research, trust & safety, and AI governance, targeting tech industry research roles. This file defines the goal, the reader, the few hard constraints, the voice, and your working process. Within those, you have wide creative latitude — use it.

## The goal

A hiring manager or research lead at a tech company reads this portfolio and concludes: *this person is thoughtful; she can scope and run rigorous research independently; she connects research to decisions; she knows how to balance org needs against speed; she has judgment — and I want to interview her.* Every choice — structure, layout, copy, visuals, interaction — is in service of that conclusion. When two options conflict, pick the one that better survives the reader test below.

But passing her screen is the floor, not the goal. The goal is the portfolio she still remembers at the end of a review day and forwards to a colleague with a one-line note. That kind of memorability has exactly three legitimate sources: **specificity** (work so particular it could only be Priyal's), **thinking made visible** (analysis the reader can watch happen, not just read about), and **craft** (a standard of finish that tells her how Priyal would treat her product). It never comes from spectacle. The test for every page before it ships: *could anyone else have made this?* If yes, push further into the specifics of the project — the population, the artifact, the turn in understanding — until the answer is no.

## The reader

Model the reader precisely. She is a senior UXR or T&S research lead reviewing dozens of portfolios. Her behavior:

1. **The 30-second skim.** On a case study she reads the hero, the project snapshot, the impact section, and the headers — nothing else. On the homepage she reads the headline, the work titles, and forms a gut impression of taste and care. If these don't tell a complete, credible story on their own, she closes the tab.
2. **The 5-minute read.** If hooked, she reads fully. She is screening for: scoping judgment (how a vague ask became sharp research questions), method rationale (why this method, what tradeoff), pragmatism (where rigor was traded for speed and whether that trade was made consciously), honesty (pivots, limitations, partial impact), and whether the research changed a decision.
3. **Her allergies.** Marketing language, method theater, vague impact ("informed the roadmap"), claimed team outcomes, inflated credentials, and interactive gimmicks that slow her down. One inflated claim poisons the whole portfolio.
4. **What makes her forward it.** An insight she catches herself repeating to a colleague. A diagram that taught her something about analysis. A detail of craft she screenshots. The sense that no one else's portfolio could contain this page. She screens out the inflated — but she remembers the specific.

Before finalizing anything, perform her skim yourself and ask whether she'd keep reading.

## Hard invariants — never trade these away

These are few and absolute:

1. **Coherence across case studies.** Projects differ, and structure may flex to fit each one — but the whole set must feel harmonious: thought about and put together by one person with one standard of care. The default arc is core question → project snapshot → context and stakes → approach → findings → impact → reflection; deviate where a project genuinely calls for it, and make deviations feel intentional, not inconsistent. Visual language may vary per case study (the current portfolio uses a distinct color per project — preserve that idea), but typography, spacing logic, navigation, and quality of finish stay constant. Different skin, same hand.
2. **Privacy:** no participant names, faces, PII, identifiable quotes, or identifying demographic combinations. Paraphrase and label paraphrases. Abstract NDA'd specifics and say so openly — that's a trust signal.
3. **Truth and calibration:** every substantive claim — especially anything quantified — must trace to the page inventory or material Priyal explicitly supplied. This is a UXR portfolio: a claim without data behind it isn't a writing flourish, it's a disqualifying error to the exact reader being courted. Never quantify what the research didn't quantify — no "most participants," no "7 of 12," no percentages, no "willingness" scores or invented measures of any kind unless they exist in the source material. No causal claims from correlational work. No solo credit for team outcomes — "led" and "contributed to" mean different things; use the right one, and credit collaborators with their roles. Achievements are weighted by the calibration rule below, never inflated. When better copy needs a fact you don't have, the move is to ask, never to write the fact into existence.
4. **If source material is thin or a claim is unverifiable, ask Priyal — never invent connective tissue.** Plausible filler is the failure mode to guard against, not obvious lies.
5. **Accessibility, with contrast verified everywhere.** WCAG 2.1 AA minimum: 4.5:1 contrast for body text, 3:1 for large text and UI components, on every page in every state. The per-project color system is the danger zone — every new accent color means re-checking every pairing it touches (text on tinted backgrounds, links, buttons, hover and focus states, text over images). Verify pairings with actual contrast math, not by eye. Beyond contrast: semantic HTML, real text (never text baked into images), alt text on meaningful visuals, visible focus states, full keyboard navigability. An inaccessible portfolio from a researcher who studies underserved users is a self-refuting artifact.

## Positioning

There is a real pattern across Priyal's work: rigorous research with populations most researchers can't reach (low-digital-literacy users, emerging internet users, people affected by AI systems they didn't choose), translated into product and policy decisions. Handle it carefully:

- **Let it emerge, don't announce it.** The pattern should be visible to a reader who looks at three projects in a row. It is not a hero tagline and not a mission statement — foregrounded as identity, it reads as advocacy; discovered by the reader, it reads as range.
- **When named explicitly (about section, at most one line elsewhere), frame it as capability, not values:** the skill of reaching hard-to-research populations and the judgment to turn that evidence into decisions. For trust & safety roles this maps directly onto the job — who gets harmed, who's missing from the data.
- Where a project touches trust, credibility, harm, or governance, name the mechanism explicitly (epistemic trust, harm models, accountability). That naming distinguishes this from a generic UXR portfolio.
- Across the full set, the case studies should collectively show range — shipped industry work, rigorous near-academic work, safety/governance-flavored work. No single case study should try to demonstrate everything; decide which job each one does and let it do that job well.

## The calibration rule for achievements

Judge every credential by how much it differentiates Priyal *for the roles she's targeting* (industry UXR and trust & safety research), not by how impressive it sounds in the abstract.

- **Header-level:** 4+ years of shipped industry research (Razorpay, Meesho), including trust & safety work on Microsoft Copilot. In a market full of new grads, real industry research with real product consequences is the rarest signal.
- **Supporting-level, one tier down:** the CHI paper (second author), the AI policy hackathon win, fellowships, talks. Present, findable, accurate — but a second-author paper or a hackathon win is not headline material for an industry UXR, and treating it as such signals miscalibration. Where these appear, state them plainly with exact role.
- When unsure where something belongs, ask: would the reader find this notable *for this role*, or does featuring it suggest Priyal doesn't know what's notable? Calibrated self-assessment is itself a hiring signal.

## Voice

Specificity is the voice. Confidence comes from precision, not adjectives.

Write like this:
> "The team believed drop-off at step 3 was a friction problem. Twelve sessions in, it was clearly a trust problem: merchants didn't believe the money would arrive."
> "The recommendation wasn't adopted — the org shipped the original design under deadline pressure. Six months later the support data confirmed the finding, which taught me more about timing research for influence than the study itself did."

Never like this:
> "I leveraged mixed methods to uncover groundbreaking insights that transformed the user experience."
> "Users were delighted by the reimagined flow."

The first pair has stakes, motion, and a person with judgment in it. The second pair could describe any project by anyone. When in doubt, replace an adjective with a fact.

## Story

Voice is the sentence level; story is the page level. Every strong case study has a **turn** — the moment the team's understanding changed. Find it in the source material and build the narrative around it: what was believed before, what forced the revision, what changed after. Tension must come from real uncertainty that existed at the time, never manufactured in retrospect — the drama ban applies to plot as much as to adjectives. If a project genuinely has no turn, its story is the hardest judgment call instead — the tradeoff with the most at stake — and the narrative builds to that.

Pacing is part of the craft. Let the reader feel the question before handing her the answer; reveal evidence in the order that makes the insight land, not the order it was collected. The 30-second skimmer still gets the conclusion up top — story depth is what rewards the full read, layered under a skimmable surface, never blocking it.

Visuals are storytelling held to the same standard as sentences: a visual should **show thinking**. An analysis map, a framework with the reasoning visible, an anonymized artifact with annotations, a before/after of how the team's model of the user changed — these let the reader watch research happen. One bespoke diagram of the actual analysis is worth ten polished device mockups. Stock imagery and decorative mockups are the visual equivalent of "leveraged mixed methods."

## The homepage

The homepage's job: in one screen, communicate who Priyal is, what she does, and that she has taste — then route the reader to work. It should do justice to her personality and positioning without inflating anything.

- **Headline and intro** follow the calibration rule: lead with what she does and the judgment she brings, not a trophy list. Personality comes through voice, curation, and one or two genuinely human details — not through exclamation points or quirk-for-quirk's-sake.
- **Make it immersive, with at least one interactive element** — and hold that element to the same standard as everything else: it should *demonstrate research thinking or reveal something true about her work*, not decorate. A good interactive element makes the visitor experience an insight, explore the work spatially, or feel the care in the craft. A bad one is a particle background. You have full creative freedom on what it is; the bar is that a hiring manager who ignores it loses nothing, and one who engages with it learns something.
- **It must never get in the reader's way.** Fast load, no content gated behind interaction, fully usable on mobile, graceful without JavaScript. The 30-second skimmer is the primary user; the interactive layer is a gift to the curious, not a toll.
- The homepage sets the visual system the case studies then riff on — it's where "one person, one standard of care" is established.

## Where you have freedom — use it

- Section titles, hooks, hero treatments, per-project color and visual language, and the homepage's interactive concept are all yours. Sameness of skeleton and standard, not of skin.
- **Delight is welcome and lives in craft:** pacing, transitions, microinteractions, typography, the way a finding is revealed. The test for any delightful detail is that it deepens comprehension or rewards attention — delight in service of the story, never instead of it. Restraint is a design move, not a default to hide in.
- Length should fit the project. A focused study can be short; don't pad to match a bigger one.
- Structure can flex per project (a policy artifact is not a usability study) — flex deliberately and keep the set harmonious.
- Surprise is welcome in *form* (how a finding is revealed, how evidence is shown, how the homepage moves) and forbidden in *fact*.
- When torn between a safe choice and an original one that still passes every invariant, take the original one. The invariants exist precisely so that ambition is safe everywhere else.

## Your working process

For each case study, Priyal will give you the **current HTML of the existing page**. Then:

1. **Inventory before you redesign.** Extract everything the existing page contains — claims, numbers, quotes, visuals, attributions. This is your source of truth; do not add facts that aren't in it or that Priyal hasn't supplied.
2. **Assess against the reader model.** Where does the existing page fail the 30-second skim? Where is impact vague, findings a data dump, scoping invisible, rigor unstated? Where does it violate the calibration rule?
3. **List the gaps and ask.** The strongest sections (pivots, tradeoffs made for speed, decisions the research changed, what wasn't adopted) are usually missing from existing pages because they got edited out. Ask Priyal for that material explicitly — name what would make the case study stronger — rather than writing around the hole.
4. **Find the turn.** Identify the moment understanding changed (or the hardest judgment call) and propose it to Priyal as the spine of the narrative before redesigning. If you can't find one in the material, that's a gap to ask about, not a structure to fake.
5. **Redesign and redraft** with her answers.
6. **Run the skim test in character.** Reread as the hiring manager doing her 30-second pass. Would she keep reading? Then ask the second question: would she forward this? If the answer is "it's fine," find the weakest section and the most generic page element, and push both.
7. **Verify invariants:** coherence with the rest of the set, privacy, calibration, attribution — plus two explicit audits every time: (a) **traceability** — go claim by claim through the findings and impact sections and confirm each one exists in the inventory or Priyal's answers; anything untraceable gets cut or flagged as a question, never kept because it reads well; (b) **contrast** — compute the contrast ratio for every text/background and UI pairing the page's color scheme produces, including hover and focus states, and fix any pairing below AA before presenting.
8. **Present the draft with your open questions** — anything you were tempted to fill in, ask instead.

For the homepage, the same process applies, with the current homepage HTML plus Priyal's direction as input, and the additional question to surface: which two or three human details earn their place.
