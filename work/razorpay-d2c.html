Razorpay

What makes merchants pay and consumers play?

A deep dive into validating a B2C rewards platform for a B2B fintech, de-risking a strategic proposal for a two-sided marketplace and unlocking a multi-million dollar investment decision.

Role

Lead UX Researcher

Duration

10 weeks, May 2024

Methods

Interviews, FGDs, large-scale survey

Outcome

Informed executive investment decision

Background

Razorpay is a 7.5 billion dollar Indian fintech solving end-to-end money movement for 8M+ businesses. It processes payments for 50,000+ merchants, all on the B2B side.

Leadership identified a strategic threat: competitors with consumer-facing (B2C) networks were starting to win B2B deals on the strength of brand recognition alone. The strategy team proposed a response: a two-sided rewards marketplace, where merchants would fund rewards that consumers could earn and redeem across brands.

Before committing, leadership needed validation. I partnered with a product strategist to design and run a study answering three questions at once: would merchants pay for this, would consumers adopt it, and where, if anywhere, did those two sets of interests actually overlap?

The research question: is there genuine demand on both sides of this marketplace, or does the concept only work on paper?

Objectives

The study was scoped around two parallel tracks, each with its own questions.

  • Merchant track: identify what would make the proposition valuable for merchants, and understand the barriers to adoption and their readiness to integrate.
  • Consumer track: measure willingness to try the platform and comfort with sharing data, and identify the trust drivers and post-purchase pain points that influence adoption.

Methodology

I developed a mixed-methods approach with parallel research streams to validate both sides of the marketplace simultaneously: in-depth participatory interviews with merchants, and a focus-group-to-survey pipeline with consumers.

1

Merchant interviews, 40+

I conducted 40+ in-depth interviews with merchant decision-makers across a stratified sample varying in company size, purchase frequency, and industry category. Each was a 50-minute semi-structured session covering pain points, a "piggy bank" exercise where merchants allocated a hypothetical budget to break down problem priorities by where they'd actually spend money, a participatory design activity for their ideal solution, an introduction of our proposal, and a direct test of willingness to pay.

The interviews found very little interest in the proposed value proposition as originally framed. But the participatory design activity was the turning point: it surfaced a different model that worked for both merchants and the company, and reframed the rest of the study.

2

Consumer focus group discussions

FGDs gave us a general user understanding and broad archetypes, which made the survey that followed far more efficient. Sessions used journey mapping, pain-point exploration through storytelling, and progressive concept testing. The group dynamic let participants build on and challenge each other's perspectives, surfacing more nuanced opinions about the D2C shopping experience than one-on-one interviews would have.

3

Consumer survey, 1000+ respondents

Partnered with a market researcher to field a survey to 1,000+ respondents, producing feature prioritization, willingness-to-try scores, a behavioral assessment for archetype creation, and a read on potential unique value.

Outcome

  • Defined key consumer personas to clarify who the proposition serves best and where adoption is most likely.
  • Identified the customer hooks that drive adoption, and which trust and post-purchase pain points needed addressing first.
  • Prioritized features into must-haves and good-to-haves, giving clear guidance for MVP scope and sequencing.
  • Reframed the merchant value proposition around what makes participation worthwhile, and where the original assumptions did not hold.
  • Narrowed the merchant segments for which the proposition is most viable, enabling a more focused go-to-market strategy.

Impact

The research directly informed the executive decision to proceed with investment. Rather than a binary go or no-go, the findings let leadership make nuanced calls about investment structure, phasing, and success metrics.

The research reframed the product strategy itself. Instead of a simple rewards platform, the concept evolved into a trust and reliability solution to help end consumers build confidence in the D2C market. Merchant segmentation and consumer adoption patterns directly shaped go-to-market strategy: instead of a broad launch, the findings supported a targeted approach focused on the merchant segments most likely to succeed.

Just as important, the collaborative research process built shared understanding across product, strategy, and executive teams. Involving stakeholders throughout data collection and synthesis created consensus around market realities that lasted beyond the project, accelerating decisions and reducing the usual friction between strategic vision and market reality.

Reflection

This project reinforced that the core value of user research lies in framing the right questions and defining what evidence is sufficient to make a decision. Rather than optimizing for exhaustive coverage, I learned the importance of designing research that is proportional to the risk being tested, and deliberately avoids premature validation.

Working across consumers and merchants also showed me how easily research can skew toward one-sided desirability if incentives aren't explicitly examined. The merchant interviews could easily have produced a polite "yes, interesting" that masked a real "no, not at this price." Designing the piggy bank and participatory exercises to get past that politeness is a technique I now reach for whenever a study risks confusing enthusiasm with intent.
