Project context

Meesho served a large audience across semi-urban and rural India.

At the time of the study, Meesho's reseller network included more than 8 million people across 700 towns. Supporting regional languages required more than translating English interface copy. The product team also needed to consider script literacy, everyday vocabulary, digital literacy, and the context in which people used each language.

I conducted a directional study to document how other companies approached these decisions and how practitioners researched vernacular-language experiences.

Research questions

The study addressed three questions.

  • 01

    How does vernacular-language support affect product engagement?

    I examined how language preferences could affect acquisition, adoption, and continued use.

  • 02

    How do companies add vernacular languages to digital products?

    I compared vernacular-first products with companies adapting an existing English-language experience.

  • 03

    How should teams conduct research with India's next billion internet users?

    I documented research preparation, sociocultural considerations, methods, ethics, and reporting practices.

Product context

The implementation approach depended on the role language played in the product.

Vernacular-first

Language is part of the value proposition

Products such as Pratilipi, ShareChat, and Vokal were designed primarily for regional-language use. Language shaped the audience, content, interaction model, and brand from the beginning.

Different starting points
Language as a feature

An existing product adds language support

Products such as Flipkart and Swiggy had to decide which journeys to adapt, validate demand, and fit regional-language content into an established product system.

Key findings

Language preference could not be inferred from spoken fluency alone.

  • 01

    Comprehension and script literacy were separate considerations.

    A person could understand a regional language without reading its script comfortably. In some contexts, transliteration in the Latin alphabet was more accessible than translation into a regional script.

  • 02

    Everyday vocabulary was often clearer than literal translation.

    Formal translations could introduce words that people rarely used in conversation. Familiar regional and English terms sometimes communicated an action more clearly.

  • 03

    Language preference changed with the task.

    People could prefer one language for communicating with other users and another for reading interface instructions or completing a transaction.

  • 04

    Teams needed to validate language choices within the product journey.

    Decisions about translation, transliteration, terminology, and script needed to be tested with the intended audience in the context of specific tasks.

Recommendations

The report translated the findings into product and research guidance.

01

Assess the need by journey

Identify which tasks would benefit from vernacular-language support instead of assuming the entire product required the same treatment.

02

Test translation and transliteration

Evaluate comprehension of language and script with the intended audience before selecting an approach.

03

Use familiar vocabulary

Prioritize words people use in everyday speech and retain familiar English terms when they are better understood.

04

Plan research for local context

Account for language, culture, digital literacy, recruitment, moderation, documentation, and research ethics.
