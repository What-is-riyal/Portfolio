Razorpay

Should a B2B fintech go consumer?

Razorpay was considering its first consumer-facing product: a rewards platform built on top of its merchant network. Before the company committed engineering and capital, leadership needed evidence that both sides of the marketplace wanted it. I led the research that informed that decision.

Role

UX Researcher, lead

Year

2023

Methods

Interviews, survey, concept evaluation

Outcome

Informed executive investment decision

Context

Razorpay built its business serving merchants: payments, banking, and lending for companies, not consumers. A D2C rewards platform would change that. It promised merchants a new channel for customer loyalty and promised consumers rewards for shopping with brands they already loved. It also meant entering a market the company had never operated in, against incumbents who had.

This was not a usability question. It was a strategy question with two unknowns: would merchants adopt the platform, and would consumers actually use it? Each side only had value if the other showed up.

The research question: does a two-sided rewards marketplace have genuine demand on both sides, or does it only look good in a pitch deck?

Approach

Because the risk lived on two sides of the marketplace, the study had two arms running in parallel: deep qualitative work with merchants, and broad quantitative validation with consumers.

1

Merchant interviews, 40+

In-depth interviews with D2C merchants across categories and sizes, probing how they currently run loyalty and retention, what they spend, where existing tools fail them, and how they reacted to the platform concept. The goal was to separate polite enthusiasm from real willingness to adopt and pay.

2

Consumer survey, 1000+ respondents

A structured survey quantifying consumer rewards behavior: which programs people join, which they actually redeem, what makes a program worth the effort, and stated interest in a cross-brand rewards platform. Survey design deliberately tested behavior, not just attitudes, to avoid the gap between what people say about rewards and what they do.

3

Synthesis for a decision, not a deck

Findings from both arms were synthesized into a single evidence base framed around the investment decision: where demand was real, where it was conditional, and what assumptions the business case rested on that the data did not support.

What the research changed

The findings gave leadership something the business case alone could not: an empirical read on whether the demand assumptions underneath the platform held up with real merchants and real consumers. The synthesis was presented directly to executives and became a core input to the company's investment decision on the platform.

For me, this project crystallized what strategic research is for. The most valuable thing a researcher can do before a company places a bet is not to validate the idea, but to find out precisely which parts of it are load-bearing.

Reflection

Two-sided marketplace research taught me to treat enthusiasm as a hypothesis, not a finding. Merchants liked the concept in the abstract; the interviews mattered because they revealed the conditions under which liking it would translate into adopting it. That discipline, designing research to surface the conditions behind the headline answer, is something I have carried into every strategic study since.
