Earning trust before automating.

What does it take for people to let an AI assistant act on their behalf, inside the most personal professional space they have: their inbox? A point-of-view paper synthesizing a year of research into one framework.

The setup

One assistant, shipping in pieces

Outlook's AI assistant was expanding fast: into task management, scheduling, email drafting, out-of-office handoffs, and inbox prioritization. Each capability was being researched and shipped somewhat independently.

I was asked to step back from any single feature and synthesize a year's worth of research across all of them into one strategic point of view: what does it actually take for people to trust an AI assistant enough to let it do more on their behalf?

I substantially authored the resulting paper, pulling separate studies into a single argument rather than a feature-by-feature report.

Why this page is dark & light The dark sections carry the framework, the light ones carry the story. The contrast is doing the chapter breaks.

The tension

People wanted a chief of staff. They were getting an intern who forgets their name.

What people wanted paraphrased from across studies

An assistant that knows my role, my priorities, and my working style, and quietly takes work off my plate.

Across every study, people described the same ideal: proactive help, in the flow of work, from something that deeply understands their context. Not another feature. A partner.

What kept happening paraphrased from across studies

Wrong names. Wrong dates. Confused context. And no explanation of why.

Small, basic errors did outsized damage. When an assistant gets simple facts wrong and can't explain itself, people don't just distrust that output. They stop trusting it with anything that matters.

Why two cards The entire paper hangs on this gap between expectation and experience. It earns its own moment before any framework appears.

The framework · an adoption ladder

1

On-demand

2

Proactive

3

Automatic

01

On-demand

You ask. It answers. You check.

The user explicitly invokes help: summarize this thread, draft a reply, find that email. Nothing happens without being asked, and every output is reviewed before it's used. This is where most people's trust actually sits today, and for many features it's the only mode they're comfortable with.

02

Proactive

It suggests. You decide.

The assistant surfaces summaries, suggestions, and priorities without being asked, but leaves every action to the user. This jump is bigger than it looks: the assistant now has to judge what's worth interrupting someone for, and explain why, or it gets dismissed as noise and switched off.

03

Automatic

It acts. Within limits you set.

The assistant sends the reply, archives the email, reschedules the meeting, on its own, inside boundaries the user has drawn. The paper's argument: this rung should only ever be extended to a task after the assistant has already proven itself on the two rungs below. Skipping rungs is how trust collapses.

Automation isn't something you design into a feature. It's something a feature earns.

The prerequisites

Three things hold the ladder up

The paper grounds the ladder in three prerequisites, drawn directly from where trust consistently broke down across the studies we synthesized.

T

Transparency

"What is it looking at, and why did it suggest that?"

People need to see what the assistant considered and why it acted. In a black box, even small mistakes generalize into "I can't trust this with anything important." With an explanation, the same mistake becomes correctable, and survivable.

C

Controllability

"Can I correct it, and set its boundaries, right here?"

Control that lives in a buried settings page functionally doesn't exist. People need to adjust boundaries and fix mistakes in the moment they happen, including deciding what the assistant may ever do without sign-off.

L

Learnability

"Is it getting better at being mine?"

Nobody expected perfection. They expected improvement: their tone, their priorities, their habits. An assistant that repeats a corrected mistake teaches people to stop correcting it, and the relationship never deepens enough to support autonomy.

Why cards tilt Micro-interactions reward attention without gating content. The framework reads fine if you never hover.

The reaction the paper kept hearing, in spirit

"It marked something high priority. But I have no idea why, so I'll check everything myself anyway."

The inbox, the emails, and these reactions are invented for illustration. The mechanic is the paper's actual recommendation: put a "why" next to every judgment the AI makes.

The framework in practice

One assistant, or five strangers wearing the same badge?

A recurring test case in the paper: when each Microsoft 365 app runs its own Copilot with its own memory, the user re-explains their role, projects, and tone in every app. That repeated re-introduction is a transparency failure and a learnability failure at once, and no single feature team can fix it alone. The framework's value was giving every team the same vocabulary to name it.

Reflection

Trust isn't a feature. It's a sequence.

Writing this paper was the first time I worked primarily as a synthesizer rather than a primary researcher. The hardest part of strategic research isn't generating new findings. It's recognizing when several studies are independently describing the same underlying problem from different angles, and naming it in a way that travels across teams.

It also crystallized something I carry directly into how I think about AI governance: trust in an automated system isn't a property you have or don't. It's built in stages, with transparency and control as the foundation, before broader autonomy is either safe or accepted. That staged view of trust is the throughline connecting my UX research background to the work I want to do next.

Why end on this A case study's last paragraph is a positioning statement. This one points at AI governance on purpose.
