SEAL IT Act: A Federal Framework for Synthetic Election Media

Priyal Shrivastava, Simi Olusola-Ajayi · 2026

First place, Deepfakes and Election Integrity track, U.S. AI Policy Hackathon (Berkeley AI Safety Initiative; 77 teams, 144 participants, three tracks) · CMU HCII article

Question

How should the United States regulate AI-generated deepfakes to protect elections while preserving First Amendment protections? States including California and Texas have passed election-deepfake laws. At the federal level, election, fraud, telecommunications, and platform rules each cover different actions, leaving gaps between the person who makes a deepfake, the platform that recommends it, and the people who redistribute it. A broad ban on false political content would raise serious constitutional problems and could reach satire and criticism.

Proposal

The proposal targets distribution, not speech. During a defined federal-election window, a covered platform temporarily stops recommending a suspected synthetic post to new audiences while it is reviewed. The post stays reachable through direct links and follower feeds.

  • Scope. Platforms with 45 million or more monthly active U.S. users.
  • Trigger. Rapidly spreading content plus credibility-weighted flags from at least 3% of engaged users, or an automated detection signal. A user-driven trigger avoids asking a government agency to select political claims for review.
  • Review. Within 24 hours, or 6 hours in the final 48 hours before polls open, using source material, cross-partisan user signals, and expert review for disputed cases.
  • Outcome. Cleared posts resume recommendation; synthetic posts are labeled; evidence of deliberate voter deception goes to election authorities. Creators can appeal, with a 24-hour response requirement and judicial review available.

Content enters the framework only if all five elements of a test are met: it is synthetic, depicts a real or presented-as-real person, lacks clear disclosure, would lead a reasonable person to believe the event occurred, and is distributed in connection with a federal election. Satire, parody, labeled synthetic content, and ordinary editing are excluded.

Responsibilities

Model developers attach C2PA content credentials and publish election-focused misuse testing before each federal cycle. Large platforms preserve provenance, run the review process, and alert other covered platforms. Creators disclose synthetic depictions of candidates. Distributors at scale preserve disclosures. Enforcement draws on FTC and FEC authority, a narrow limit on platform immunity for knowing continued amplification, and penalties for deliberate voter deception.

Open questions

The proposal has not been piloted or reviewed by counsel. A pilot would need to establish whether a 3% flag threshold identifies fast-moving risk without excessive false alarms, whether platforms can meet the review deadlines, how often review stages disagree, how error rates differ across media formats and demographic groups, and how much content evades the framework through watermark removal, private messaging, or smaller platforms.
