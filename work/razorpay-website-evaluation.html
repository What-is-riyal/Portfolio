Razorpay

Moving from tech-first to human-first language on a website with 1M+ daily views

A mixed-method evaluative study of Razorpay's redesigned homepage, testing brand perception, usability, and comprehension before the new design went live to over a million daily visitors.

Role

UX Researcher

Duration

6 weeks, November 2023

Stakeholders

Product designers, PM, comms, marketing

Outcome

10% lift in conversion

Background

Razorpay is a 7.5 billion dollar Indian fintech solving end-to-end money movement for 8M+ businesses. For most of its history, the company was known primarily for its payments gateway, even as it expanded into neobanking, payroll, and lending.

To shift brand perception toward something more relatable for smaller, less tech-savvy businesses, the Razorpay homepage, visited by 1M+ people every day, went through a redesign. I proposed a research study to address the critical need for comprehensive testing before a revamp at this scale shipped.

Objectives

  • Test the new brand positioning through the website.
  • Test relatability and comprehension through visuals and content.
  • Identify usability issues in the new design.

Methodology

A mixed-method approach ensured actionable findings for each team involved: content, visual communication, and product design. Alongside a premortem and a UX audit, I ran a series of small, targeted tests with 10 participants, business owners not currently using Razorpay, over remote, blinded sessions of about 50 minutes each, using Lookback, Google Meet, Figma prototypes, and Typeform.

1

Design premortem

A workshop with design, content, and product stakeholders to pre-empt where the new design could fail. This surfaced current knowledge gaps and hypotheses to test in the rounds that followed.

2

UX audit

An internal design audit with product designers across the organization, to identify hygiene issues with the design and prototype before involving external participants.

3

Impression test

What: exposed a stripped-down version of the first fold for 10 seconds.
Why: to identify the most noticeable elements of the first fold.
How: stopped the screen share after 10 seconds and asked participants what they remembered seeing.

4

Visual perception test

What: free exploration of a static version of the website with all text replaced by Lorem Ipsum.
Why: to isolate the impact of visuals alone on brand perception and comprehension.
How: participants explored a static Figma prototype, then described what they thought the company did and who its audience might be, and chose 5 of 12 visual descriptive words (a mix of relevant and irrelevant terms) to describe the visual language.

5

Usability test

What: free-access usability followed by guided exploration.
Why: to understand final brand perception, identify usability issues, and test specific hypotheses from the premortem.
How: participants explored a Figma prototype with real copy and interactions, described what they thought the company did, completed a product discovery task, rated the brand on three levers, chose descriptive brand words, and gave a System Usability Scale (SUS) score.

6

Preference test

What: a side-by-side comparison with the previous website.
Why: to identify and measure improvement directly.
How: participants explored the old site, described what was different between the two versions, repeated the brand-perception and word-choice tasks, and gave their preference with a reason.

Impact

10%

Increase in overall website conversion

90%

Suggestions incorporated across content, design and branding

Through the redesign, the team had gotten caught up in "look and feel" and strayed from product comprehension. This research raised fundamental questions about a few core elements of the website and pushed for their redesign before launch.

The detailed findings from this study are under NDA. If you'd like to know more, reach out.

Reflection

This project is a good example of why "usability" and "brand" can't be tested in isolation on a page this trafficked. A homepage redesign is as much a comprehension problem as a navigation problem: if a visitor can't tell what the company does or who it's for within the first ten seconds, no amount of polished interaction design will recover that visit. Layering the impression test, visual perception test, and usability test in sequence let each one isolate a different variable, visuals alone, then visuals with copy, then full interaction, so that when something failed, we knew exactly which layer it failed at.
