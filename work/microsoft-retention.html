Why people were leaving Outlook on the mobile web

Usage of Outlook's lightweight mobile browser experience was declining among consumer users, and everyone on the team had a different explanation for it. This was a mixed-methods study, 36 interviews followed by a 300+ response validation survey, that replaced those competing theories with an account of where this product actually sits in people's lives.

This was a team study led by Khyati Seth. I joined as a contract researcher: I contributed to the discussion guide, ran interview sessions alongside her, led the qualitative analysis under her guidance, and wrote the first drafts of the report that she refined. The segmentation and study design credit belongs to the team. Because the product and its findings are under NDA, this page has no internal artifacts on it.

Context

The product in question is the version of Outlook people reach through a mobile browser rather than the installed app. Its consumer usage was declining faster than expected, and leadership, product, and engineering each had a theory about why: the app was simply better, notifications were missing, the experience was too sparse. The theories were plausible, mutually incompatible, and all held with confidence. The study's first job was to separate what the team believed from what it knew.

Starting from what was already known

Before fielding anything, the team pooled the existing evidence: data science reports, telemetry, past UX audits, marketing research, and earlier studies. That synthesis was presented to the whole team, data scientists, PMs, and designers included, in a hypothesis workshop. The decision to start this way was deliberate and saved the study from two common failures: paying participants to answer questions telemetry could already answer, and designing a discussion guide around whichever stakeholder spoke loudest.

What survived the workshop, the questions no existing data could answer, became the research plan: what drives loyalty and churn for each kind of user, what value this endpoint holds next to the app and the desktop, and an early read on the investments the team was already considering, such as multi-account support, a home-screen shortcut, and notifications. The workshop also produced six user segments, drawn from real usage behavior across Outlook's endpoints, which structured recruitment and analysis for everything downstream.

The study itself

The design was qualitative first, quantitative second: interviews to find the story, a survey to check it before anyone acted on it.

  • 36 moderated interviews. One-on-one sessions with 4 to 6 participants from each of the six segments, recruited against actual usage patterns rather than self-description. Leaders and PMs were invited to listen in live, which meant the findings started doing their work weeks before any report existed.
  • A validation survey, 300+ responses. The interview findings were mapped into a survey instrument and fielded across the cohorts. The purpose was discipline: the qualitative story was compelling, and compelling stories are exactly the ones worth checking against larger numbers before a team plans a semester around them.

The analysis lens

Two ideas from the analysis shaped how the team came to talk about the product. I can describe them at the level of pattern, without the findings underneath.

The first is that email accounts have life cycles. People rarely have one email account; they have a small system of them, and each account sits somewhere on a path from primary (checked daily, guarded carefully, attached to everything that matters) through secondary (signups and backups) to a junk drawer of newsletters and one-time codes, and finally to dormancy, where an account is opened once a year for a password reset. An account's position in this cycle predicts how, and how often, any product will be used to reach it. Product teams tend to design for the primary account; much of real usage involves the others.

The second is routing. Loyalty to a favorite email client barely appeared in the interviews. What appeared instead was people matching the endpoint to the account's job: a full app for the account they live in, a quick browser tab for the account they only visit for a code. Seen through that lens, the mobile web experience had a role nobody had been designing for. It functions as a buffer: it catches people when the main app is slow, unsynced, or not installed, and it serves people on storage-constrained phones who cannot afford a heavy app. Part of its retention therefore depends on products it doesn't control, which is precisely why "why are people leaving?" could not be answered by examining this product in isolation. Choosing the user's whole account system as the unit of analysis, rather than the single product, was the most consequential decision in the study.

What the study changed

  • Leadership and PMs heard users directly, by sitting in on live interviews rather than waiting for a readout.
  • Each function got a deep-dive readout walking through the sections relevant to its decisions, rather than one generic presentation for everyone.
  • The top findings were presented to leadership in India and to the global Outlook teams at headquarters.
  • Beyond the deck, the insights were handed over as a working tracker that PMs used for semester planning. Most of the insights in it influenced the next semester's plan for the mobile web experience.

Reflection

The lesson I keep from this study is about choosing the unit of analysis early. It is tempting to treat retention as a property of the product, something to fix with onboarding or a redesigned inbox. For a product that lives inside someone's decades-old email ecosystem, the honest unit of analysis is that ecosystem, and committing to it early shaped the discussion guide, the segments, and what the team ultimately did with the results.

The other thing I value in hindsight is the shared vocabulary the analysis produced. The life-cycle and routing language let a cross-functional team discuss a person who checks a near-dormant account once a year and a person who lives in their inbox daily without flattening them into a single persona. That vocabulary stayed in use long after the readouts ended, which is a better measure of a study's impact than the deck itself.
