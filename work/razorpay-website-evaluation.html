Razorpay · 2023

Evaluating a homepage redesign before a million people saw it

Razorpay was about to replace its homepage, a page with over a million daily visitors, with a redesign that traded technical language for a warmer, human voice. No research was planned for the launch. I proposed the study, designed it as a series of small tests that each isolated one way the new page could fail, and delivered the findings in time to act on them. Conversion rose 10% after the tested redesign shipped, and 90% of the recommendations were adopted.

Role
UX Researcher; proposed, designed, and ran the study
Timeline
6 weeks, November 2023, before launch
Stakeholders
Design, content, and product, plus comms and marketing
Participants
10 business owners, none current Razorpay users, in blinded remote sessions
Outcome
10% lift in website conversion; 90% of recommendations adopted

Detailed findings are under NDA. The study design, the reasoning behind each instrument, and the outcomes are shown.

Context

Razorpay built its name on a payments gateway, and its homepage looked like it: dark, technical, dense with product terminology. As the company expanded into neobanking, payroll, and lending, the brand needed to feel approachable to smaller, less technical businesses. The redesign led with a photograph of a small business owner and the line "Payments made easy for the ones who solve the hard problems."

The redesign was a brand and design initiative moving quickly toward a launch date, and no evaluation had been scoped. I proposed one, on a simple argument: a page this trafficked, changing this much, deserved evidence that the new warmth didn't cost it comprehension. The specific risk I was worried about was that in pursuing look and feel, the page would stop answering the three questions every first-time visitor brings to it: what does this company do, is it for someone like me, and can I trust it with money?

Because nobody had asked for the study, winning agreement for it, scoping it inside a fixed deadline, and making it useful to five different teams was as much the work as running it.

Why a battery of small tests, rather than one usability test

A single usability test would have returned a single verdict: people like the page or they don't. That isn't actionable when a content team, a visual design team, and a product team each own a different slice of the page. So I built the study in layers, each isolating one variable, so that when something failed we would know which layer it failed at and which team could fix it: what the visuals alone communicate, what changes when the real copy is added, what breaks when people actually use the page, and whether the new page beats the old one side by side.

The six instruments, and why each one

Two internal rounds ran first, so that participant time was spent only on questions participants could uniquely answer. Then ten business owners, none of them current Razorpay users, went through the layered tests in blinded 50-minute remote sessions, against Figma prototypes, with structured responses captured in Typeform.

1. Design premortem, internal

A workshop with design, content, and product stakeholders that imagined the launch had already failed and worked backward to why. The clustered failure modes became the specific hypotheses the later tests were built to check. This also did quiet political work: every team saw its own fears written into the test plan, which made them co-authors of the study rather than its audience. I credit a large part of the eventual 90% adoption rate to this session.

2. UX audit, internal

A heuristic review of the prototype with product designers from across the organization, so external sessions wouldn't be spent rediscovering problems a designer could spot in an afternoon. Issues were logged and fixed before any participant saw the page.

3. Impression test, ten seconds

Participants saw the first fold for exactly ten seconds, then listed everything they remembered. Ten seconds is roughly the attention a real first-time visitor gives a homepage, so whatever fails to register in that window effectively isn't on the page, however much the team debated it. This told us precisely which first-fold elements were carrying their weight.

4. Visual perception test

Participants freely explored the full page with every word replaced by placeholder text, then described what they thought the company did and who it was for, and chose five descriptive words from a list of twelve. The de-worded page isolates what the visual language communicates on its own, before copy can compensate for it. One detail mattered here: the word list deliberately mixed on-brand and off-brand terms, because participants offered only flattering words will pick flattering words.

5. Usability test, with SUS

Free exploration and guided tasks on the full prototype, with real copy and interactions. This layer tested the premortem's specific hypotheses in context: participants described what the company did, completed a product discovery task, rated the brand on three levers, repeated the word-choice task, and gave a System Usability Scale score.

6. Preference test, against the old page

Finally, participants explored the current homepage, articulated the differences in their own words, repeated the brand-perception and word-choice tasks, and stated a preference with a reason. Improvement was the claim the redesign was making, so the study measured it directly instead of assuming it.

What it led to

The layered structure made the central drift visible and specific: through the redesign process, the page had moved toward look and feel and away from product comprehension. Because each finding arrived tied to a layer, it also arrived pre-addressed to the team that could act on it. Several core elements of the page were redesigned before launch rather than after.

  • After the tested redesign shipped, overall website conversion rose 10%.
  • 90% of the study's recommendations were adopted across content, design, and branding.

Of the two numbers, the adoption rate is the one I watch. Findings ship as a function of how a study is socialized, not just how it is run. The premortem gave every team a stake in the answers, and delivering findings team by team, in each team's language, against the fears they had named themselves, meant the report never had to fight for attention.

Reflection

Research you propose is a different job from research you are handed. The deadline was fixed, the teams were already moving, and the study had to earn its place by being cheap in calendar time and generous in what it returned to each stakeholder. Scoping six instruments into six weeks, with the internal rounds absorbing the work participants shouldn't have to do, was the stakeholder management as much as the methodology.

The other lesson was about closing the loop under a deadline. A report nobody reads before launch is indistinguishable from no research at all. I delivered findings while there was still time to act on them, and that is where the 90% adoption figure comes from: a study built from the first day to be acted on inside someone else's schedule.
