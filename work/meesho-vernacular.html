Meesho · 2020

Language and bias in research for India's next billion users

Meesho's users are largely women from semi-urban and rural India, running reselling businesses on an app designed, and mostly researched, in English. As a research intern, I led a ten-week study on what that gap costs: what translation flattens, what transliteration misses, and which biases creep in when teams build for users they rarely meet. The report gave the design and product teams a working vocabulary for language decisions, and one finding became its own workshop.

Role
User Research Intern; led the study end to end
Timeline
May – July 2020, remote, mid-lockdown
Participants
10 practitioners: 5 researchers, 4 designers, 1 product manager
Sessions
About 70 minutes each, against 3 role-specific questionnaires
Output
Research report, plus a stakeholder workshop on practitioner bias

Context

At the time of the study, vernacular-language users were projected to make up nearly three quarters of India's internet by 2021, and rural users were already averaging more minutes online per week than urban ones. Meesho sat directly on top of this shift: a social commerce platform with more than ten million downloads, whose resellers were mostly more comfortable in Hindi and regional languages than in English. The product, and most of the research behind it, still ran on English defaults. The open question was what that was costing, in comprehension, in trust, and in how far research findings could be believed when the sessions themselves ran across a language gap.

Why expert interviews

The obvious design, sessions with end users, was the wrong one for the constraints. It was mid-2020, everything was remote, I had ten weeks, and the users in question were precisely the people hardest to reach well over a video call in a second language. A thin version of that fieldwork would have produced thin findings with confident formatting.

Instead, I interviewed the people who had already spent years doing that fieldwork: ten practitioners from across the industry who research and design for vernacular users. Expert interviews trade firsthand contact for accumulated experience, and the trade is honest as long as the report says clearly that this is what it did. Mine does.

Each interview ran about 70 minutes, against one of three questionnaires built for how each role meets the language problem:

  • Five UX researchers, on running sessions across languages: moderation, translation loss, building rapport, and how findings shift with the language of the interview itself.
  • Four designers, on vernacular interfaces: script and layout, icon comprehension, and where translated UI text breaks a design.
  • One product manager, on the business case: when vernacular investment pays off, and how teams decide which languages to support first.

The central finding

The experts kept converging on the same distinction: translation is not the same as speaking someone's language. Translating an interface into textbook Hindi often makes it harder to use, because nobody shops in textbook Hindi. Transliterating English words into Devanagari script keeps familiar vocabulary but can read as noise. What users actually speak is a colloquial mix, familiar borrowed English words kept as they are, with the language around them in the user's own register.

The report's position followed from that: go hybrid rather than pure. Neither full translation nor full transliteration serves these users; a colloquial mixed-language approach does.

What else the report argued

  • Language is a research variable, not logistics. The language a session runs in changes what participants say and how confident they feel saying it. Discussion guides and analysis need to account for it explicitly.
  • Socio-cultural context outweighs vocabulary. Trust signals, family dynamics around purchases, and who in the household owns the phone shaped behavior more than any single string of UI text.
  • Practitioner bias is the quiet failure mode. Teams defaulted to imagining low-tech-literacy users as less capable rather than differently experienced. This finding felt too important to leave in a document, so it became a dedicated workshop with design and product stakeholders, naming the biases out loud.

Reflection

I was an intern, and this was a ten-week study built on expert interviews and secondary research rather than fieldwork with end users; its impact was a vocabulary and a set of considerations the teams could use, not a shipped feature. I'd rather describe it at that scale than inflate it. It still set the direction of my career: the question of who research quietly excludes has followed me into every role since.

What it taught me about method has lasted too. The conditions of research, the language of the session, who feels entitled to speak, what the participant assumes the researcher wants to hear, are method decisions, not logistics. A discussion guide that is flawless in English can be a different instrument entirely in Hindi, and pretending otherwise produces confident findings about the wrong thing.
