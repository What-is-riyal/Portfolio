The question

Should a B2B fintech bet on consumers?

Razorpay's leadership was weighing a strategic pivot: build a consumer-facing rewards marketplace on top of a payments business that had never had to think about end consumers. Before committing real investment, they needed to know if demand for this existed on either side of the marketplace, or only in the pitch deck.

Background

A B2B fintech, eyeing a B2C flank

Razorpay is a large Indian fintech built around B2B payments infrastructure: moving money for tens of thousands of businesses, none of whom are individual consumers. Its competitors don't all look like that. Some of them also run consumer-facing networks, brands and apps that ordinary people use directly, and that consumer footprint was starting to help them win B2B deals on name recognition alone.

Leadership's response was a proposal: a two-sided rewards marketplace, where merchants would fund rewards that consumers could earn and redeem across participating brands. On paper, it solved two problems at once, giving Razorpay a consumer touchpoint and giving merchants a new acquisition channel.

Before committing budget and headcount, leadership needed to know whether either side of that marketplace actually wanted it. I partnered with a product strategist to design a study that could answer that for both sides at once, and tell the difference between polite interest and a real "yes."

Findings, segment names, and the eventual product direction are under NDA. The research questions, method, and what the process surfaced are shown.

The shape of the study

Two questions, asked at the same time

A marketplace fails if either side doesn't show up. Validating only the merchant side, or only the consumer side, would have told leadership half of what they needed and left the riskier half as an assumption. So the study ran as two parallel streams that fed into the same decision.

Merchant track

Would merchants pay for this?

What would make a rewards marketplace valuable enough for a merchant to fund, and what would stop them from integrating at all.

  • Where does this rank against a merchant's other priorities and budget lines?
  • What would make the value proposition worth paying for, versus a nice-to-have?
  • What's the real barrier to integration: cost, effort, trust, or something else?

Consumer track

Would consumers download this?

Whether end consumers would try a new rewards platform, what would earn their trust, and where their existing shopping experience already breaks down.

  • How comfortable are people sharing data and payment details with a new platform?
  • What pain points in D2C shopping today create an opening for something new?
  • Which features would actually move someone from curious to willing to try?

Method

Three instruments, run in sequence

Each method fed the next. The merchant interviews ran on their own track and ended up reshaping the whole study. The consumer side moved from open-ended group discussions to a large survey, using what the groups surfaced to make the survey sharper rather than broader.

1

Merchant interviews, 25+

I ran 25+ in-depth, 50-minute interviews with merchant decision-makers, stratified by company size, purchase frequency, and industry. Each session covered current pain points, a hypothetical-budget exercise (the "piggy bank," below), a participatory activity where merchants sketched their own ideal solution, and then a direct test of the proposed rewards marketplace.

The pitch itself landed flat. What changed the study was the activity that came right before it.

2

Consumer focus group discussions, 3

Three FGDs gave us broad consumer archetypes and a shared vocabulary for the D2C shopping experience, using journey mapping, pain-point storytelling, and progressive concept testing. Group dynamics let participants challenge each other's takes, which surfaced more honest reactions to the rewards concept than one-on-one interviews tend to.

3

Consumer survey, 1000+ respondents

Working with a market research partner, I fielded a survey to 1,000+ respondents to quantify what the FGDs surfaced: feature prioritization, willingness-to-try scores, a behavioral read for archetype-building, and a check on whether the concept offered anything genuinely new.

The turn, part one

Where would a merchant actually put ₹100?

Asked directly, "would you pay for a rewards marketplace?", merchants tended to say something polite and noncommittal. So before the pitch, every interview opened with a different question: if you had a fixed budget to spend on fixing problems in your business, where would it go? Try it yourself below, then see where merchants actually put their money.

Across the interviews, the rewards platform consistently came last. Merchants put their hypothetical rupees toward problems they already felt: payments failing silently, fraud they had to absorb, and support tickets that sat unanswered. A consumer rewards programme was the one line item that read as "nice to have" rather than "I am losing money over this."

"I don't think about my customer's customer. I think about whether this transaction goes through and who I call when it doesn't."

This exercise didn't kill the project. It redirected it. The gap between "fund a rewards programme" and "fix the things that are actually costing me money" became the question the rest of the study tried to close, through the participatory activity that followed.

The turn, part two

From a rewards platform to a trust layer

After the budget exercise, each merchant interview included a participatory design activity: sketch your own version of a solution to the problems you just ranked, no constraints. Merchants kept drawing the same thing, in different words: something that made transactions and disputes with their own customers more predictable and visible. Not a marketing channel. A trust mechanism, sitting underneath the transaction itself.

That reframing changed what the consumer track was testing too. The consumer survey and prototype work stopped asking "would you join a rewards programme" and started probing what would make someone trust a D2C purchase enough to complete it, which mapped directly onto what merchants had just sketched.

The original pitch

"A rewards marketplace merchants fund and consumers redeem across brands."

A new acquisition channel for merchants, a new reason for consumers to download an app. Validated on its own terms, it tested as polite indifference on both sides.

What the research surfaced

"A trust and reliability layer that makes D2C transactions feel safe to complete."

Built from what merchants sketched unprompted and what made consumers hesitate before buying. The same underlying need, described independently by both sides of the marketplace.

Synthesis

Closing the loop with both teams

Because the same study served two stakeholder groups inside Razorpay, the synthesis was run as a joint workshop with product, strategy, and the merchant-facing team in the room together. Findings from each track were laid out side by side, so the overlap (and the gaps) were visible to everyone making the call, not just summarized in a deck afterward.

Outcome

A decision, not a verdict

The study didn't return a simple go or no-go. It gave leadership a clearer map of where real demand sat on each side of the marketplace, and what would need to be true for the original pitch to work at all.

  • aAn informed investment decision. Leadership could decide on investment structure, phasing, and success metrics against evidence from both sides, instead of a single up-front bet.
  • bA reframed value proposition. The concept moved from a rewards marketplace to a trust-and-reliability layer for D2C transactions, grounded in what both merchants and consumers independently described.
  • cA targeted go-to-market. Merchant segmentation from the interviews narrowed where the proposition was viable, supporting a focused launch into the segments most likely to adopt rather than a broad rollout.
  • dShared ground across teams. Because product, strategy, and the merchant team synthesized together, the read on market reality held beyond the readout, reducing the usual friction between strategy and what research found.

What I learned

Proportional research, and the cost of premature validation

This project sharpened how I think about scoping a study to the size of the decision it informs. A multi-million dollar strategic bet doesn't need exhaustive coverage of every possible angle; it needs evidence that's proportional to the risk, aimed squarely at the assumptions the decision actually depends on. Validating the original pitch in isolation would have been faster, and would have validated the wrong thing.

The piggy bank exercise also taught me something about politeness as a research hazard. Asked directly, merchants would have told us the rewards platform sounded interesting, a "yes" that masked a real "not at this price, not for this problem." Designing instruments that get past politeness, before the pitch is even on the table, is a technique I now build into any study where the stakes of a false positive are high.
